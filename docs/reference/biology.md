## Anatomy & Physiology

### Topic 1: Skeletal and Muscular Systems

**Q1.** `[C]` List the five primary functions of the skeletal system.
> **Answer:** (1) Support — structural framework. (2) Protection — shields vital organs. (3) Movement — provides attachment points for muscles. (4) Mineral storage — stores calcium and phosphorus. (5) Hematopoiesis — blood cell production in red bone marrow.

**Q2.** `[C]` Differentiate between compact (cortical) bone and spongy (cancellous) bone in terms of structure and location.
> **Answer:** Compact bone: dense, lamellar structure arranged around Haversian canals; forms the outer shell of bones. Spongy bone: trabecular (lattice) network, contains red marrow; found at epiphyses and flat bones. Compact bone provides strength; spongy reduces weight while maintaining strength.

**Q3.** `[C]` Explain the roles of osteoblasts, osteoclasts, and osteocytes in bone remodeling.
> **Answer:** Osteoblasts: bone-forming cells; secrete collagen and minerals to build bone matrix. Osteoclasts: bone-resorbing cells; secrete acids and enzymes to break down bone (important in calcium homeostasis). Osteocytes: mature osteoblasts embedded in lacunae of the matrix; sense mechanical stress and coordinate remodeling.

**Q4.** `[P]` Identify whether each joint type allows which range of motion: hinge, ball-and-socket, pivot, saddle.
> **Answer:** Hinge (e.g., elbow, knee): flexion/extension only (uniaxial). Ball-and-socket (e.g., hip, shoulder): multiaxial (flexion, extension, abduction, adduction, rotation). Pivot (e.g., atlas-axis, radioulnar): rotation only (uniaxial). Saddle (e.g., thumb CMC): biaxial (flexion/extension + abduction/adduction).

**Q5.** `[P]` Distinguish between the axial and appendicular skeleton. List three bones in each division.
> **Answer:** Axial: skull, vertebrae, ribs, sternum, hyoid. Appendicular: humerus, femur, radius, ulna, tibia, fibula, clavicle, scapula, pelvic girdle.

**Q6.** `[CT]` A patient with osteoporosis has low bone density. Predict the effects on osteoblast/osteoclast activity and suggest the mechanism by which calcium supplementation + Vitamin D could help.
> **Answer:** In osteoporosis, osteoclast activity exceeds osteoblast activity. Calcium supplementation provides raw material for bone matrix. Vitamin D (calcitriol) promotes intestinal calcium absorption and stimulates osteoblast activity, shifting balance toward bone formation.

**Q7.** `[N]` Why does the periosteum play a critical role in fracture repair even though it is not actually bone tissue?
> **Answer:** The periosteum contains osteoprogenitor cells (osteoblast precursors) and blood vessels. In fracture repair, these cells proliferate and differentiate into active osteoblasts that form the callus (new bone tissue) bridging the fracture. Without the periosteum, fracture healing would be severely impaired.

**Q8.** `[C]` List the three types of muscle tissue and compare their structural and functional characteristics.
> **Answer:** (1) Skeletal: striated, voluntary, multinucleated (syncytium from myoblast fusion). (2) Cardiac: striated, involuntary, mononucleated, connected by intercalated discs with gap junctions. (3) Smooth: non-striated, involuntary, mononucleated, found in walls of hollow organs.

**Q9.** `[P]` Describe the sliding filament theory of muscle contraction, naming each molecular player.
> **Answer:** (1) Calcium ($Ca^{2+}$) released from SR binds to troponin (on actin filament). (2) Troponin-tropomyosin complex shifts, exposing myosin-binding sites on actin. (3) Myosin heads bind actin, forming cross-bridges. (4) Power stroke: myosin heads pivot (using ADP+Pi → releasing Pi), pulling actin toward center of sarcomere. (5) ATP binds myosin, breaking the cross-bridge. (6) ATP hydrolysis re-cocks myosin head. Cycle repeats while $Ca^{2+}$ is present.

**Q10.** `[P]` What is rigor mortis? Explain it in terms of the sliding filament theory.
> **Answer:** Rigor mortis is the temporary stiffening of muscles after death. After death, ATP production ceases. Myosin heads bind actin but cannot release (ATP is needed to break the cross-bridge). Muscles remain locked in contraction until enzymatic breakdown of myosin/actin occurs (typically 48–72 hours post-mortem).

**Q11.** `[CT]` A runner sustains a tear of the Achilles tendon (calcaneal tendon). Which muscle is affected, and how will this impact their biomechanics?
> **Answer:** The Achilles tendon connects the gastrocnemius and soleus (calf) muscles to the calcaneus (heel bone). Plantar flexion is lost or severely impaired. The runner cannot push off the ground effectively, eliminating the critical propulsive phase of the gait cycle.

**Q12.** `[N]` Explain how isometric contraction, isotonic concentric contraction, and isotonic eccentric contraction differ — and give a real-world example of each.
> **Answer:** Isometric: muscle generates force without changing length (e.g., holding a heavy box still). Isotonic concentric: muscle shortens while generating force (e.g., bicep curl lifting phase). Eccentric: muscle lengthens while generating force (e.g., bicep curl lowering phase). Eccentric contractions cause more muscle damage and DOMS.

### Topic 2: Nervous System and Action Potentials

**Q13.** `[C]` Distinguish between the central nervous system (CNS) and peripheral nervous system (PNS). Include subdivisions of the PNS.
> **Answer:** CNS: brain + spinal cord. PNS: all nervous tissue outside CNS. Subdivisions: (1) Sensory (afferent) — carries signals to CNS. (2) Motor (efferent): (a) Somatic — voluntary, skeletal muscle; (b) Autonomic — involuntary: sympathetic and parasympathetic.

**Q14.** `[C]` Compare sympathetic and parasympathetic effects on heart rate, digestion, and pupil size.
> **Answer:** Sympathetic ("fight or flight"): increases HR, decreases digestion, dilates pupils. Parasympathetic ("rest and digest"): decreases HR, stimulates digestion, constricts pupils.

**Q15.** `[P]** Describe the ionic events during each phase of an action potential, referencing the channels involved.
> **Answer:** (1) Resting (−70 mV): K⁺ leak channels open, Na⁺/K⁺ pump maintains gradient. (2) Depolarization: stimulus opens voltage-gated Na⁺ channels; Na⁺ rushes in → inside becomes positive (reaches +35 mV). (3) Repolarization: Na⁺ channels inactivate; voltage-gated K⁺ channels open; K⁺ rushes out. (4) Hyperpolarization: K⁺ channels remain open briefly, making inside more negative than resting. (5) Return to resting: K⁺ channels close; Na⁺/K⁺ pump restores gradients.

**Q16.** `[P]** What is the refractory period? Distinguish between absolute and relative refractory periods.
> **Answer:** Absolute refractory period: Na⁺ channels are inactivated; no action potential can be generated regardless of stimulus strength. Ensures directionality of propagation. Relative refractory period: K⁺ channels are still open; a suprathreshold stimulus can trigger an action potential. Both periods follow directly from the kinetics of voltage-gated channels.

**Q17.** `[CT]` How does myelination speed up action potential conduction? What happens in diseases like multiple sclerosis (MS)?
> **Answer:** Myelination insulates the axon; action potentials can only occur at nodes of Ranvier, allowing saltatory conduction (jumping from node to node) — much faster than point-by-point conduction. In MS, the myelin sheath is attacked by the immune system, disrupting saltatory conduction and causing slowed, blocked, or scrambled signals.

**Q18.** `[CT]` Tetrodotoxin (TTX) blocks voltage-gated Na⁺ channels. Predict the effect of TTX on: (a) action potential generation, (b) muscle contraction, (c) sensory perception.
> **Answer:** (a) Action potentials cannot be generated (depolarization phase blocked). (b) No motor neuron signals → no muscle contraction → paralysis. (c) Sensory neurons also cannot fire → complete numbness. TTX poisoning causes paralysis and can be fatal if respiratory muscles are affected.

**Q19.** `[P]** Describe how a chemical synapse works, from action potential in the presynaptic neuron to response in the postsynaptic cell.
> **Answer:** (1) AP arrives at axon terminal. (2) Voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels open; Ca²⁺ influx. (3) Ca²⁺ triggers synaptic vesicle fusion with presynaptic membrane (exocytosis). (4) Neurotransmitter released into synaptic cleft. (5) NT binds to receptors on postsynaptic membrane. (6) Ion channels open/close → EPSP or IPSP in postsynaptic cell. (7) NT is removed by reuptake, enzymatic degradation, or diffusion.

**Q20.** `[N]` SSRI antidepressants block serotonin reuptake. Using your knowledge of synapse mechanics, explain how this elevates serotonin signaling.
> **Answer:** Normally, serotonin is removed from the synaptic cleft by reuptake into the presynaptic neuron via the serotonin transporter (SERT). By blocking SERT, SSRIs leave serotonin in the cleft longer, allowing it to continue binding postsynaptic receptors and prolonging/strengthening its signaling effect.

**Q21.** `[N]` Explain temporal and spatial summation of postsynaptic potentials.
> **Answer:** Temporal summation: rapid repeated firing from one presynaptic neuron releases NT before the previous EPSP has fully decayed, causing additive effect. Spatial summation: multiple presynaptic neurons fire simultaneously onto the same postsynaptic cell, and their individual EPSPs sum to potentially reach threshold.

### Topic 3: Endocrine System

**Q22.** `[C]` Compare endocrine and exocrine glands. Give two examples of each.
> **Answer:** Endocrine: ductless, secrete hormones into blood (e.g., thyroid, adrenal cortex, pancreatic islets, pituitary). Exocrine: secrete via ducts onto body surfaces or into cavities (e.g., sweat glands, salivary glands, pancreatic acinar cells).

**Q23.** `[C]` Explain the hypothalamic-pituitary axis. What is a tropic hormone?
> **Answer:** Hypothalamus releases releasing hormones (e.g., TRH) that stimulate the anterior pituitary to release tropic hormones (e.g., TSH). Tropic hormones target other endocrine glands (e.g., TSH targets the thyroid). The axis uses negative feedback loops to maintain homeostasis.

**Q24.** `[P]` For each hormone, state where it is produced, its target, and its primary effect: Insulin, Glucagon, Cortisol, Aldosterone, ADH, Growth Hormone.
> **Answer:**
> - **Insulin** (β-cells of islets of Langerhans) → Liver, muscle, adipose: lowers blood glucose (promotes uptake/storage).
> - **Glucagon** (α-cells) → Liver: raises blood glucose (promotes glycogenolysis, gluconeogenesis).
> - **Cortisol** (adrenal cortex) → Multiple: raises blood glucose, anti-inflammatory, stress response.
> - **Aldosterone** (adrenal cortex) → Kidney collecting duct: increases Na⁺ reabsorption, K⁺ excretion (raises BP).
> - **ADH** (posterior pituitary) → Kidney collecting duct: increases water reabsorption (reduces urine volume).
> - **Growth Hormone** (anterior pituitary) → Liver, bone, muscle: stimulates growth and IGF-1 production.

**Q25.** `[CT]` A patient has chronically high blood glucose, polyuria, polydipsia, and weight loss despite increased appetite. Distinguish between Type 1 and Type 2 diabetes mellitus based on the mechanism.
> **Answer:** Type 1: autoimmune destruction of β-cells → no insulin produced → cells cannot take up glucose → high blood glucose. Body breaks down fat/protein for energy (weight loss). Polyuria because glucose spills into urine (osmotic diuresis). Type 2: cells develop insulin resistance; β-cells compensate but eventually fail. Often associated with obesity, not autoimmune.

**Q26.** `[CT]` Explain how negative feedback regulates thyroid hormone (TH) levels. What happens in primary hypothyroidism?
> **Answer:** Low TH → hypothalamus releases TRH → anterior pituitary releases TSH → thyroid produces TH → TH inhibits hypothalamus and pituitary (negative feedback). In primary hypothyroidism (thyroid gland fails): TH is low, TSH is high (pituitary trying harder but thyroid not responding).

**Q27.** `[N]` Acromegaly results from excess growth hormone in adults. Why doesn't it cause increases in long bone length, but does cause enlargement of the jaw, hands, and feet?
> **Answer:** In adults, the epiphyseal plates (growth plates) have fused, preventing longitudinal bone growth. Excess GH instead causes periosteal bone deposition (thickening and enlargement) in facial bones, hands, and feet, which can continue even in adults.

**Q28.** `[N]` Explain why the pancreas is described as having both endocrine and exocrine functions.
> **Answer:** Endocrine: Islets of Langerhans (α, β, δ cells) secrete glucagon, insulin, and somatostatin directly into blood. Exocrine: Acinar cells secrete digestive enzymes (lipase, amylase, proteases) via the pancreatic duct into the duodenum. These are anatomically and functionally separate.

### Topic 4: Cardiovascular System

**Q29.** `[C]` Trace the complete pathway of blood through the heart, naming all four chambers, valves, and major vessels (both pulmonary and systemic circuits).
> **Answer:** Deoxygenated blood from body → Superior/inferior vena cava → Right atrium → Tricuspid valve → Right ventricle → Pulmonary semilunar valve → Pulmonary arteries → Lungs (gas exchange) → Pulmonary veins (4) → Left atrium → Mitral (bicuspid) valve → Left ventricle → Aortic semilunar valve → Aorta → Systemic circulation.

**Q30.** `[P]` Describe the cardiac cycle (systole and diastole) and relate it to the Korotkoff sounds heard during blood pressure measurement.
> **Answer:** Systole (ventricles contract): atrioventricular valves close → 1st heart sound ("lub"). Semilunar valves open → blood ejected. Diastole (ventricles relax): semilunar valves close → 2nd heart sound ("dub"). AV valves open → ventricles fill. Korotkoff sounds: systolic BP is when sounds first appear (turbulent flow); diastolic is when sounds disappear (flow becomes laminar).

**Q31.** `[P]` Define stroke volume, heart rate, and cardiac output. State the relationship between them.
> **Answer:** Stroke volume (SV): volume ejected per beat. Heart rate (HR): beats per minute. Cardiac output (CO): $CO = HR \times SV$. At rest: CO ≈ 5 L/min (HR = 70 bpm, SV = 70 mL). During exercise, both HR and SV increase, raising CO to 20+ L/min.

**Q32.** `[P]` Identify the components of an ECG trace (P, QRS, T waves) and explain what cardiac electrical event each represents.
> **Answer:** P wave: atrial depolarization (atria contract). QRS complex: ventricular depolarization (ventricles contract); atrial repolarization is hidden here. T wave: ventricular repolarization (ventricles relax). PR interval: AV node delay.

**Q33.** `[CT]` A patient presents with peripheral edema (swelling in legs), shortness of breath, and jugular vein distension. Which side of the heart has failed, and why?
> **Answer:** Right heart failure. The right ventricle cannot pump blood effectively into the pulmonary circulation. Blood backs up into the venous system → elevated venous pressure → fluid leaks into tissues (edema), JVD, liver congestion. Left heart failure causes pulmonary edema (fluid in lungs).

**Q34.** `[CT]` Explain how the baroreceptor reflex maintains blood pressure homeostasis after standing up quickly (orthostatic challenge).
> **Answer:** Standing → gravity reduces venous return → blood pressure drops → baroreceptors (in aortic arch and carotid sinus) detect reduced stretch → less inhibitory tone on vasomotor center → sympathetic activation: increases HR, increases contractility, causes vasoconstriction → BP restored. Failure of this reflex causes orthostatic hypotension (lightheadedness on standing).

**Q35.** `[N]` Explain why the left ventricle has a much thicker wall than the right ventricle, despite pumping the same stroke volume.
> **Answer:** The left ventricle pumps against systemic vascular resistance (~100 mmHg), while the right pumps against pulmonary resistance (~25 mmHg — much lower). Greater pressure work requires more muscular wall. Wall thickness reflects pressure load, not volume.

**Q36.** `[N]` What is the Frank-Starling law? How does it allow the heart to automatically match output from both ventricles?
> **Answer:** Frank-Starling law: the more the ventricle is stretched by filling (preload), the stronger the subsequent contraction (up to a limit). If one ventricle pumps slightly more than the other, the receiving ventricle fills more, stretches more, and automatically increases its stroke volume to match output — ensuring cardiac output of both ventricles stays equal.

### Topic 5: Respiratory System

**Q37.** `[C]` Describe the complete pathway of air from the atmosphere to the alveoli.
> **Answer:** Nasal cavity (filter, warm, humidify) → Pharynx → Larynx (epiglottis protects airway) → Trachea → Primary bronchi → Secondary (lobar) bronchi → Tertiary (segmental) bronchi → Bronchioles → Terminal bronchioles → Respiratory bronchioles → Alveolar ducts → Alveolar sacs → Alveoli (gas exchange).

**Q38.** `[P]** Explain the mechanism of breathing using Boyle's Law.
> **Answer:** Boyle's Law: $P \propto 1/V$ at constant temperature. Inspiration: diaphragm contracts and flattens → lung volume increases → pressure inside lungs drops below atmospheric → air flows in. Expiration: diaphragm relaxes → volume decreases → pressure rises above atmospheric → air flows out.

**Q39.** `[P]` Define tidal volume, vital capacity, residual volume, and functional residual capacity.
> **Answer:** Tidal volume (TV): volume per normal breath (~500 mL). Vital capacity (VC): maximum exhaled after maximum inhalation (~4.8 L). Residual volume (RV): air remaining after maximum exhalation (~1.2 L); lungs never fully collapse. Functional residual capacity (FRC): air in lungs after passive exhalation = ERV + RV (~2.5 L).

**Q40.** `[CT]` Explain how $\text{CO}_2$ is transported in the blood. What is the bicarbonate buffer system?
> **Answer:** CO₂ transport: (1) ~7% dissolved in plasma. (2) ~23% bound to hemoglobin as carbaminohemoglobin. (3) ~70% as bicarbonate via: $\text{CO}_2 + \text{H}_2\text{O} \xrightarrow{\text{carbonic anhydrase}} \text{H}_2\text{CO}_3 \rightarrow \text{H}^+ + \text{HCO}_3^-$. HCO₃⁻ enters plasma; H⁺ is buffered by hemoglobin. Bicarbonate buffer: $\text{HCO}_3^-/\text{H}_2\text{CO}_3$ maintains blood pH at ~7.4.

**Q41.** `[CT]` A patient has blood pH 7.25, $P_{CO_2}=55\text{mmHg}$, and normal $HCO_3^-$. What is the acid-base disorder and what is the likely cause?
> **Answer:** Respiratory acidosis. Low pH (acidotic), elevated $P_{CO_2}$ (hypercapnia), normal $HCO_3^-$ (no metabolic compensation yet — acute). Cause: hypoventilation (e.g., COPD, opioid overdose, neuromuscular disease). Treatment: correct the underlying cause; in severe cases, mechanical ventilation.

**Q42.** `[N]` At high altitude, $P_{O_2}$ is lower. Explain both the immediate and long-term adaptations that allow the body to maintain adequate oxygen delivery.
> **Answer:** Immediate: increased ventilation rate (hypoxic drive via peripheral chemoreceptors), increased heart rate. Long-term (acclimatization): increased EPO (erythropoietin) secretion by kidney → increased RBC production (polycythemia) → more hemoglobin → more O₂ carrying capacity. Also: rightward shift of oxyhemoglobin dissociation curve (increased 2,3-BPG), more capillary growth in muscles.

### Topic 6: Digestive and Urinary Systems

**Q43.** `[C]` List the organs of the alimentary canal in order. Identify the site of each major class of nutrient absorption.
> **Answer:** Mouth → Esophagus → Stomach → Small intestine (duodenum → jejunum → ileum) → Large intestine → Rectum → Anus. Absorption: most carbohydrates and proteins → jejunum. Fats (as micelles) → jejunum. Vitamin B12 and bile salts → ileum. Water and electrolytes → large intestine.

**Q44.** `[P]** What digestive enzymes are secreted by the pancreas? What are their substrates and products?
> **Answer:** Pancreatic amylase (starch → maltose). Pancreatic lipase (triglycerides → fatty acids + glycerol). Trypsin, chymotrypsin, elastase (proteins → smaller peptides — activated from zymogens). Carboxypeptidase (polypeptides → amino acids). Pancreatic ribonuclease/DNase (nucleic acids → nucleotides).

**Q45.** `[CT]** A patient lacks bile (e.g., blocked bile duct). How would this affect fat digestion and absorption? What symptom would result?
> **Answer:** Bile emulsifies fats, breaking them into small droplets for lipase access. Without bile: fat digestion is severely impaired → fat malabsorption → steatorrhea (fatty, foul-smelling stools). Fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K) are also malabsorbed → deficiency symptoms.

**Q46.** `[N]` Explain why the villi and microvilli of the small intestine are functionally important. Estimate the increase in surface area they provide.
> **Answer:** The mucosa would provide a small surface area; villi and microvilli (brush border) dramatically increase surface area for absorption. The total small intestine surface area is ~250 m² (comparable to a tennis court), compared to ~0.6 m² without these folds — a ~400-fold increase.

**Q47.** `[P]` Trace the path of filtrate through a nephron, naming the sections and what is reabsorbed in each.
> **Answer:** Glomerulus (filtration of water, ions, glucose, urea) → Bowman's capsule → Proximal convoluted tubule (reabsorbs ~65% water, Na⁺, glucose, amino acids — all glucose normally) → Loop of Henle (descending: water exits; ascending: Na⁺, Cl⁻, K⁺ exit — impermeable to water) → Distal convoluted tubule (secretion of H⁺, K⁺; regulated Na⁺ reabsorption) → Collecting duct (water reabsorption regulated by ADH; H⁺ secretion) → Renal pelvis → Ureter → Bladder.

**Q48.** `[CT]` Explain the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS) and how it responds to low blood pressure.
> **Answer:** Low BP → kidneys release renin → converts angiotensinogen (liver) to Angiotensin I → ACE (in lungs) converts to Angiotensin II → (1) causes vasoconstriction (raises BP), (2) stimulates aldosterone release (adrenal cortex) → kidney retains Na⁺ and water → increased blood volume → BP rises. This is why ACE inhibitors (e.g., lisinopril) lower BP.

**Q49.** `[N]` A patient with diabetes insipidus (deficient ADH) produces large volumes of very dilute urine. Trace the physiological pathway explaining why.
> **Answer:** ADH normally acts on the collecting duct to insert aquaporin-2 channels, allowing water reabsorption into the concentrated medullary interstitium. Without ADH: no aquaporins inserted → collecting duct remains impermeable to water → water stays in filtrate and is excreted → very large volumes (>3 L/day) of dilute urine (low osmolality).

### Topic 7: Immune, Lymphatic, and Reproductive Systems

**Q50.** `[C]` Differentiate innate and adaptive immunity. Give two examples of each.
> **Answer:** Innate: non-specific, no memory, immediate. Examples: skin barrier, phagocytosis (neutrophils, macrophages), NK cells, complement system, fever. Adaptive: antigen-specific, has memory, slower first response but faster on re-exposure. Examples: T-lymphocytes (cell-mediated), B-lymphocytes/antibodies (humoral).

**Q51.** `[C]` Explain the difference between MHC class I and MHC class II molecules and which cells express each.
> **Answer:** MHC I: expressed on ALL nucleated body cells; presents intracellular antigens (e.g., viral peptides) to cytotoxic T cells (CD8⁺) → triggers killing of infected cells. MHC II: expressed only on professional antigen-presenting cells (dendritic cells, macrophages, B cells); presents extracellular antigens to helper T cells (CD4⁺) → activates adaptive immune response.

**Q52.** `[CT]` Explain the clonal selection theory and how it accounts for immunological memory.
> **Answer:** Each B or T cell has a unique antigen receptor. When an antigen enters, it selectively activates (clonally selects) the lymphocytes with matching receptors. These proliferate into: (1) Effector cells (antibody-secreting plasma cells or cytotoxic T cells) — for immediate response, and (2) Memory cells — long-lived cells that persist after infection clears. On re-exposure, memory cells mount a faster, stronger (secondary) immune response.

**Q53.** `[N]` Explain how vaccines exploit immunological memory to prevent disease.
> **Answer:** Vaccines present antigens (killed/weakened pathogens, proteins, or mRNA encoding proteins) without causing disease. This triggers the primary adaptive immune response, generating memory B and T cells specific to the pathogen. On natural exposure, the secondary response is rapid and potent enough to eliminate the pathogen before symptoms develop.

**Q54.** `[CT]` Why are organ transplant recipients given immunosuppressive drugs? What are the risks?
> **Answer:** The recipient's immune system recognizes the donor organ's foreign MHC proteins as non-self and mounts an immune attack (rejection). Immunosuppressants (e.g., cyclosporine, tacrolimus) inhibit T cell activation to prevent rejection. Risks: increased susceptibility to infections and malignancies due to suppressed immune surveillance.

**Q55.** `[P]` Describe the function of the lymphatic system in maintaining fluid homeostasis.
> **Answer:** About 3 L/day of fluid filtered from capillaries into tissue spaces fails to return via venous osmosis. Lymphatic capillaries (highly permeable) collect this excess interstitial fluid (lymph), proteins, and immune cells. Lymph flows through lymph nodes (immune surveillance/filtering) via lymphatic vessels, ultimately returning to the bloodstream via the thoracic duct and right lymphatic duct at the subclavian veins.

**Q56.** `[N]` Explain edema at the molecular level. Give two different pathological mechanisms that could cause it.
> **Answer:** Edema = accumulation of fluid in interstitial space. Starling forces govern: osmotic pressure draws fluid in; hydrostatic pressure pushes it out. (1) Low plasma proteins (hypoalbuminemia — e.g., liver failure, malnutrition): osmotic pressure drops → fluid leaves capillaries. (2) Elevated venous hydrostatic pressure (e.g., right heart failure, deep vein thrombosis): more fluid pushed out than can return. (3) Lymphatic obstruction (lymphedema): fluid cannot drain.

**Q57.** `[P]** Trace the path of a sperm cell from the site of production to fertilization.
> **Answer:** Produced in seminiferous tubules (testes) → Epididymis (maturation and storage) → Vas deferens → Ejaculatory duct (joined by seminal vesicle duct) → Urethra (passing through prostate, which adds fluid) → Expelled from body → Travel through vagina → Cervix → Uterus → Fallopian tube → Fertilization (usually in the ampulla of the fallopian tube).

**Q58.** `[P]` Describe the phases of the female menstrual cycle, naming hormones controlling each phase.
> **Answer:** (1) Menstrual phase (days 1-5): low estrogen/progesterone → endometrium sheds. (2) Follicular phase (days 1-14): FSH stimulates follicle development → rising estrogen → endometrium rebuilds. (3) Ovulation (day 14): LH surge (triggered by peak estrogen) → follicle ruptures → egg released. (4) Luteal phase (days 15-28): LH causes corpus luteum to form → secretes progesterone (+estrogen) → maintains endometrium for implantation. If no fertilization, corpus luteum degenerates → hormone levels drop → menstruation.

**Q59.** `[CT]` Explain the immunological paradox of pregnancy: why doesn't the mother's immune system reject the fetus (which is semi-allogeneic)?
> **Answer:** The placenta creates an immunologically privileged environment through several mechanisms: (1) Trophoblasts express non-classical HLA molecules (HLA-G) that suppress NK cell cytotoxicity. (2) Local immunosuppressive cytokines (IL-10, TGF-β) shift toward immune tolerance. (3) The mother shifts from Th1 (cytotoxic) to Th2 (humoral) immunity. Failure of these mechanisms can contribute to pregnancy complications like preeclampsia.

**Q60.** `[N]` A patient is given a corticosteroid (like prednisone) for an inflammatory condition. Predict the effects on: blood glucose, immune function, bone density, and the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis.
> **Answer:** Blood glucose: increases (cortisol promotes gluconeogenesis). Immune function: suppressed (cortisol is anti-inflammatory, reduces lymphocyte activity). Bone density: decreases (cortisol inhibits osteoblasts and reduces calcium absorption). HPA axis: negative feedback suppresses ACTH and CRH. If prednisone is stopped abruptly, the patient may experience adrenal insufficiency because the HPA axis has been suppressed.
