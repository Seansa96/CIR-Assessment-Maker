# Chapters 6 & 8: Applications of Integration

## Introduction to Geometric Applications

In Calculus I, the definite integral was rigorously defined as the limit of Riemann sums to solve one specific geometric problem: finding the area under a curve. However, the true power of the definite integral lies in its ability to accumulate *any* continuously varying quantity over an interval. In this chapter, we generalize the Riemann sum framework to solve complex geometric problems (areas between curves, volumes of irregular solids, lengths of curves, and surface areas) and physical problems (work, hydrostatic force, and centers of mass). 

For advanced students, we will also explore higher-dimensional generalizations, parameterized curves, and Pappus's Centroid Theorems, which frequently appear in advanced calculus competitions.

## 6.1 Areas Between Curves

### 6.1.1 The General Area Formula
Consider a region $S$ that lies between two continuous curves $y = f(x)$ and $y = g(x)$, and between the vertical lines $x = a$ and $x = b$. If we partition the interval $[a, b]$ into $n$ subintervals of width $\Delta x$ and choose sample points $x_i^*$ in each subinterval, we can approximate the area of $S$ using a Riemann sum of rectangles with height $|f(x_i^*) - g(x_i^*)|$:
$$ A \approx \sum_{i=1}^n |f(x_i^*) - g(x_i^*)| \Delta x $$

Taking the limit as $n \to \infty$ gives the exact area:
> **Area Between Curves:**
> $$ A = \int_a^b |f(x) - g(x)| \, dx $$

In practice, to drop the absolute value bars, we must determine which curve is the "upper" curve and which is the "lower" curve on the interval. If $f(x) \ge g(x)$ for all $x \in [a, b]$, then:
$$ A = \int_a^b [f(x) - g(x)] \, dx $$

If the curves cross each other, the interval $[a, b]$ must be split at the intersection points, and the integral must be evaluated piecewise.

### 6.1.2 Integrating with Respect to $y$
Some regions are bounded by curves that are more easily described as functions of $y$, such as $x = f(y)$ and $x = g(y)$. In these cases, we slice the region with *horizontal* rectangles.
If $f(y) \ge g(y)$ for all $y \in [c, d]$ (meaning $f(y)$ is the "right" curve and $g(y)$ is the "left" curve):
$$ A = \int_c^d [f(y) - g(y)] \, dy $$

### 6.1.3 Worked Examples

**Example 1: Basic Area**
Find the area of the region bounded by $y = e^x$, $y = x$, and bounded on the sides by $x = 0$ and $x = 1$.

*Solution:*
Since $e^x > x$ for all $x \ge 0$, the upper curve is $y = e^x$.
$$ A = \int_0^1 (e^x - x) \, dx = \left[ e^x - \frac{1}{2}x^2 \right]_0^1 $$
$$ = \left(e^1 - \frac{1}{2}(1)^2\right) - \left(e^0 - 0\right) = e - \frac{1}{2} - 1 = e - \frac{3}{2} $$

**Example 2: Intersecting Curves**
Find the area of the region enclosed by the parabolas $y = x^2$ and $y = 2x - x^2$.

*Solution:*
First, find the points of intersection by setting the equations equal:
$$ x^2 = 2x - x^2 \implies 2x^2 - 2x = 0 \implies 2x(x - 1) = 0 $$
The curves intersect at $x = 0$ and $x = 1$.
On the interval $[0, 1]$, let's test $x = 1/2$:
$y_1 = (1/2)^2 = 1/4$
$y_2 = 2(1/2) - (1/2)^2 = 1 - 1/4 = 3/4$
So $y = 2x - x^2$ is the upper curve.
$$ A = \int_0^1 [(2x - x^2) - x^2] \, dx = \int_0^1 (2x - 2x^2) \, dx $$
$$ = \left[ x^2 - \frac{2}{3}x^3 \right]_0^1 = 1 - \frac{2}{3} = \frac{1}{3} $$

**Example 3: Horizontal Slicing**
Find the area enclosed by the line $y = x - 1$ and the parabola $y^2 = 2x + 6$.

*Solution:*
If we slice vertically, the lower boundary changes from the bottom half of the parabola to the line. Slicing horizontally is much easier.
Solve for $x$: 
Line: $x = y + 1$
Parabola: $x = \frac{1}{2}y^2 - 3$
Find intersections:
$$ y + 1 = \frac{1}{2}y^2 - 3 \implies y^2 - 2y - 8 = 0 \implies (y-4)(y+2) = 0 $$
The intersections are at $y = -2$ and $y = 4$.
Testing $y = 0$, the line is at $x=1$ and the parabola is at $x=-3$. The line is the "right" curve.
$$ A = \int_{-2}^4 \left[ (y + 1) - \left(\frac{1}{2}y^2 - 3\right) \right] \, dy = \int_{-2}^4 \left(-\frac{1}{2}y^2 + y + 4\right) \, dy $$
$$ = \left[ -\frac{1}{6}y^3 + \frac{1}{2}y^2 + 4y \right]_{-2}^4 = \left( -\frac{64}{6} + 8 + 16 \right) - \left( \frac{8}{6} + 2 - 8 \right) = 18 $$

## 6.2 Volumes: Slicing, Disks, and Washers

### 6.2.1 General Volume by Slicing
To find the volume of any solid $S$, we can define an axis (say, the $x$-axis) from $a$ to $b$. Let $A(x)$ be the area of the cross-section of $S$ in the plane perpendicular to the $x$-axis at point $x$. If $A(x)$ is continuous, the volume is:
$$ V = \int_a^b A(x) \, dx $$

### 6.2.2 Solids of Revolution (Disk Method)
If a solid is generated by revolving a region under a curve $y = f(x)$ about the $x$-axis, the cross-sections perpendicular to the $x$-axis are solid circular disks.

**Derivation via Riemann Sums:**
Partition the interval $[a, b]$ into $n$ subintervals of width $\Delta x$. Approximate the region under the curve with rectangles of height $f(x_i^*)$ and width $\Delta x$. When revolved around the $x$-axis, each rectangle sweeps out a solid cylinder (a disk) with radius $r = f(x_i^*)$ and thickness $h = \Delta x$.
The volume of one such disk is $V_i = \pi r^2 h = \pi [f(x_i^*)]^2 \Delta x$.
Summing the volumes of all $n$ disks gives a Riemann sum approximation of the total volume:
$$ V \approx \sum_{i=1}^n \pi [f(x_i^*)]^2 \Delta x $$
Taking the limit as $n \to \infty$ produces the exact volume integral.

<!-- [PLACEHOLDER: This explanation requires the generation of media showing a 2D rectangle under a curve being revolved into a 3D cylindrical disk, emphasizing the radius f(x) and thickness dx.] -->

> **Disk Method:**
> $$ V = \pi \int_a^b [f(x)]^2 \, dx $$

### 6.2.3 Solids of Revolution (Washer Method)
If the region being revolved is bounded between two curves $y = f(x)$ (outer) and $y = g(x)$ (inner), revolving it about the $x$-axis creates cross-sections that resemble washers (a disk with a hole).

**Geometric Analysis:**
A washer is formed by taking a large disk of radius $R(x) = f(x)$ and removing a smaller concentric disk of radius $r(x) = g(x)$.
The cross-sectional area of the washer is the area of the outer disk minus the area of the inner disk:
$A(x) = \pi [R(x)]^2 - \pi [r(x)]^2 = \pi ([f(x)]^2 - [g(x)]^2)$.

Integrating this cross-sectional area yields the total volume.

<!-- [PLACEHOLDER: This analysis requires the generation of media showing a cross-sectional washer, clearly labeling the outer radius R(x), the inner radius r(x), and highlighting the solid area between them.] -->

> **Washer Method:**
> $$ V = \pi \int_a^b \left( [f(x)]^2 - [g(x)]^2 \right) \, dx $$
*(Warning: The formula is NOT $[f(x) - g(x)]^2$! Doing so would incorrectly calculate the volume of a disk with radius $f(x)-g(x)$, rather than subtracting the volumes).*

### 6.2.4 Worked Examples

**Example 4: The Disk Method**
Find the volume of the solid obtained by rotating the region bounded by $y = \sqrt{x}$ and $y=0$ from $x=0$ to $x=4$ about the $x$-axis.

*Solution:*
The radius is $R(x) = \sqrt{x}$.
$$ V = \pi \int_0^4 (\sqrt{x})^2 \, dx = \pi \int_0^4 x \, dx = \pi \left[ \frac{1}{2}x^2 \right]_0^4 = 8\pi $$

**Example 5: The Washer Method about a Non-Axis Line**
Find the volume of the solid obtained by rotating the region bounded by $y = x$ and $y = x^2$ about the line $y = 2$.

*Solution:*
The curves intersect at $x=0$ and $x=1$. We are revolving around $y=2$.
The outer radius $R(x)$ is the distance from $y=2$ to the *lower* curve $y=x^2$: $R(x) = 2 - x^2$.
The inner radius $r(x)$ is the distance from $y=2$ to the *upper* curve $y=x$: $r(x) = 2 - x$.
$$ V = \pi \int_0^1 \left( (2 - x^2)^2 - (2 - x)^2 \right) \, dx $$
$$ = \pi \int_0^1 \left( (4 - 4x^2 + x^4) - (4 - 4x + x^2) \right) \, dx = \pi \int_0^1 (x^4 - 5x^2 + 4x) \, dx $$
$$ = \pi \left[ \frac{1}{5}x^5 - \frac{5}{3}x^3 + 2x^2 \right]_0^1 = \pi \left( \frac{1}{5} - \frac{5}{3} + 2 \right) = \frac{8\pi}{15} $$

## 6.3 Volumes by Cylindrical Shells

Sometimes slicing perpendicular to the axis of revolution results in washers that are extremely difficult to express or integrate. In these cases, we slice *parallel* to the axis of revolution, sweeping out nested cylindrical shells.

### 6.3.1 The Shell Method Formula
Consider a cylindrical shell with radius $r$, height $h$, and thickness $\Delta r$. If we "unroll" this shell, it forms a rectangular slab. 
The length of this slab is the circumference of the cylinder, $2\pi r$. Its height is $h$, and its thickness is $\Delta r$. 
Its volume is approximately $V \approx (2\pi r)(h)(\Delta r) = 2\pi r h \Delta r$.

**Derivation via Riemann Sums:**
If we rotate a region bounded by $y=f(x)$ and $y=0$ from $x=a$ to $x=b$ ($0 \le a < b$) about the $y$-axis, we can partition $[a,b]$ into $n$ subintervals. A vertical rectangular strip at $x_i^*$ with width $\Delta x$ and height $f(x_i^*)$ is revolved about the $y$-axis, sweeping out a cylindrical shell.
The radius of this shell is $x_i^*$, its height is $f(x_i^*)$, and its thickness is $\Delta x$.
The total volume is the limit of the sum of these shells:
$$ V = \lim_{n \to \infty} \sum_{i=1}^n 2\pi x_i^* f(x_i^*) \Delta x $$

<!-- [PLACEHOLDER: This explanation requires the generation of media showing a vertical rectangle being revolved around the y-axis to form a hollow cylindrical shell, then conceptually "unrolling" it into a flat rectangular slab.] -->

> **Cylindrical Shells Method:**
> $$ V = \int_a^b 2\pi x f(x) \, dx $$

If revolving around an arbitrary vertical line $x = L$, the radius of the shell becomes the horizontal distance from the slice at $x$ to the axis $L$. This distance is either $(x - L)$ if the region is to the right of the axis, or $(L - x)$ if the region is to the left.

### 6.3.2 Worked Examples

**Example 6: Using Shells Instead of Washers**
Find the volume of the solid obtained by rotating the region bounded by $y = 2x^2 - x^3$ and $y = 0$ about the $y$-axis.

*Solution:*
The curve intersects the $x$-axis at $x = 0$ and $x = 2$.
Attempting the Washer method would require solving $y = 2x^2 - x^3$ for $x$ in terms of $y$, which requires the cubic formula (a nightmare).
Using cylindrical shells: radius is $x$, height is $2x^2 - x^3$.
$$ V = \int_0^2 2\pi x (2x^2 - x^3) \, dx = 2\pi \int_0^2 (2x^3 - x^4) \, dx $$
$$ V = 2\pi \left[ \frac{1}{2}x^4 - \frac{1}{5}x^5 \right]_0^2 = 2\pi \left( 8 - \frac{32}{5} \right) = \frac{16\pi}{5} $$

## 6.4 Work and Physical Applications

### 6.4.1 Work Done by a Variable Force
In physics, if an object moves along a straight line with position function $x(t)$ under the influence of a variable force $F(x)$, the total work done in moving the object from $x=a$ to $x=b$ is:
$$ W = \int_a^b F(x) \, dx $$

**Hooke's Law for Springs:**
The force required to maintain a spring stretched $x$ units beyond its natural length is $F(x) = kx$, where $k$ is the spring constant.

**Example 7: Hooke's Law**
A force of 40 N is required to hold a spring stretched from its natural length of 10 cm to a length of 15 cm. How much work is done in stretching the spring from 15 cm to 18 cm?

*Solution:*
First, find $k$. The stretch is $x = 15 - 10 = 5 \text{ cm} = 0.05 \text{ m}$.
$F = kx \implies 40 = k(0.05) \implies k = 800 \text{ N/m}$.
The work done from $x = 0.05 \text{ m}$ to $x = 0.08 \text{ m}$ is:
$$ W = \int_{0.05}^{0.08} 800x \, dx = \left[ 400x^2 \right]_{0.05}^{0.08} = 400(0.0064 - 0.0025) = 400(0.0039) = 1.56 \text{ Joules} $$

### 6.4.2 Pumping Liquids from a Tank
To find the work required to pump a liquid out of a tank, we slice the liquid horizontally into thin layers. We calculate the work required to lift each layer to the top, and integrate.
$W = \int_{bottom}^{top} (\text{Density}) \cdot (\text{Gravity}) \cdot (\text{Area of slice at } y) \cdot (\text{Distance lifted}) \, dy$

## 8.1 Arc Length

### 8.1.1 The Arc Length Formula
To find the length of a curve $y = f(x)$ from $x=a$ to $x=b$, we approximate the curve with polygonal segments. The length of a small segment $ds$ is $\sqrt{(dx)^2 + (dy)^2} = \sqrt{1 + (dy/dx)^2} dx$.
If $f'$ is continuous on $[a, b]$, the exact length is:
> **Arc Length:**
> $$ L = \int_a^b \sqrt{1 + [f'(x)]^2} \, dx $$

If the curve is described as $x = g(y)$, $c \le y \le d$, then:
$$ L = \int_c^d \sqrt{1 + [g'(y)]^2} \, dy $$

**Example 8: Arc Length**
Find the exact length of the curve $y = \frac{1}{3}(x^2+2)^{3/2}$ from $x=0$ to $x=3$.

*Solution:*
$y' = \frac{1}{3} \cdot \frac{3}{2} (x^2+2)^{1/2} (2x) = x\sqrt{x^2+2}$. 
Square it: $(y')^2 = x^2(x^2+2) = x^4+2x^2$. 
Add 1: $1 + (y')^2 = 1 + 2x^2 + x^4 = (x^2+1)^2$. (Notice this forms a perfect square, a very common trick in textbook arc length problems).
$$ L = \int_0^3 \sqrt{(x^2+1)^2} \, dx = \int_0^3 (x^2+1) \, dx = \left[ \frac{x^3}{3} + x \right]_0^3 = (9+3) - 0 = 12 $$

## 8.2 Area of a Surface of Revolution

A surface of revolution is formed when a curve is rotated about a line. 
The lateral surface area $S$ is obtained by integrating the circumference of a cross-sectional band $2\pi r$ times its slant width $ds = \sqrt{1 + (dy/dx)^2} dx$:

**Rotation about the $x$-axis (radius is $y$):**
$$ S = \int_a^b 2\pi y \sqrt{1 + \left(\frac{dy}{dx}\right)^2} \, dx $$

**Rotation about the $y$-axis (radius is $x$):**
$$ S = \int_a^b 2\pi x \sqrt{1 + \left(\frac{dy}{dx}\right)^2} \, dx $$

## 8.3 Applications to Physics and Engineering

### 8.3.1 Hydrostatic Force
The pressure $P$ at depth $d$ in a fluid with mass density $\rho$ is $P = \rho g d$. 
The hydrostatic force $F$ exerted by the fluid on a submerged vertical plate is the integral of pressure over the area:
$$ F = \int_a^b \rho g \cdot d(y) \cdot w(y) \, dy $$
where $d(y)$ is the depth of the strip at height $y$, and $w(y)$ is its width.

### 8.3.2 Moments and Centers of Mass
For a continuous thin plate (lamina) with uniform density $\rho$ covering a region $D$ bounded by $y=f(x)$, $y=0$, $x=a$, and $x=b$:
The total mass is $m = \rho \int_a^b f(x) \, dx$.
The moment about the $y$-axis is $M_y = \rho \int_a^b x f(x) \, dx$.
The moment about the $x$-axis is $M_x = \rho \int_a^b \frac{1}{2} [f(x)]^2 \, dx$.
The center of mass $(\bar{x}, \bar{y})$, also called the centroid, is:
$$ \bar{x} = \frac{M_y}{m} = \frac{1}{A} \int_a^b x f(x) \, dx $$
$$ \bar{y} = \frac{M_x}{m} = \frac{1}{A} \int_a^b \frac{1}{2} [f(x)]^2 \, dx $$

## 8.9 Advanced Applications (100+1 Problems)

In Olympiad-level calculus, classical geometric formulas are generalized to higher dimensions or applied creatively using powerful theorems to circumvent brutal integrations.

### 8.9.1 Pappus's Centroid Theorems
Pappus's theorems provide an incredibly elegant way to compute volumes and surface areas of solids of revolution without performing any integration, provided the centroid is known (or vice versa).

**First Theorem of Pappus (Surface Area):**
The surface area $S$ of a surface of revolution generated by rotating a plane curve $C$ about a non-intersecting axis in the same plane is equal to the product of the arc length of $C$ and the distance traveled by the centroid of $C$.
$$ S = 2\pi \bar{y} L $$
*(where $\bar{y}$ is the distance from the centroid of the curve to the axis of rotation).*

**Second Theorem of Pappus (Volume):**
The volume $V$ of a solid of revolution generated by rotating a plane region $R$ about a non-intersecting axis in the same plane is equal to the product of the area of $R$ and the distance traveled by the centroid of $R$.
$$ V = 2\pi \bar{y} A $$

**Example 9: Volume of a Torus**
Find the volume of a torus (a donut) generated by rotating the circle $x^2 + (y-R)^2 = r^2$ about the $x$-axis (where $R > r$).

*Solution via Pappus:*
We could use the washer method, which requires integrating $\pi \int ((R + \sqrt{r^2-x^2})^2 - (R - \sqrt{r^2-x^2})^2) dx$. This is tedious.
Instead, use Pappus's Second Theorem.
The region $R$ is a circle of radius $r$. Its area is $A = \pi r^2$.
The centroid of a circle is its geometric center. For $x^2 + (y-R)^2 = r^2$, the center is $(0, R)$.
The distance from the centroid to the axis of rotation ($x$-axis) is exactly $R$.
The distance traveled by the centroid is $2\pi R$.
$$ V = (\text{Area}) \cdot (\text{Distance traveled by centroid}) = (\pi r^2)(2\pi R) = 2\pi^2 r^2 R $$

### 8.9.2 Higher-Dimensional Volumes of Revolution
A frequent topic in advanced calculus is generalizing volumes to $n$-dimensions using the Gamma function.
For instance, the volume of an $n$-dimensional hypersphere of radius $R$ is given by:
$$ V_n = \frac{\pi^{n/2}}{\Gamma(n/2 + 1)} R^n $$
For $n=2$ (a circle): $V_2 = \pi^1 / \Gamma(2) R^2 = \pi R^2$.
For $n=3$ (a sphere): $V_3 = \pi^{3/2} / \Gamma(5/2) R^3 = \frac{\pi\sqrt{\pi}}{\frac{3}{2} \frac{1}{2} \sqrt{\pi}} R^3 = \frac{4}{3}\pi R^3$.
