1 · Constants, charge, vectors

1.1 · Electrostatic constants

ke=14πϵ0=8.99×109,ϵ0=8.85×10−12k_e=\frac1{4\pi\epsilon_0}=8.99\times10^9,\quad \epsilon_0=8.85\times10^{-12}ke​=4πϵ0​1​=8.99×109,ϵ0​=8.85×10−12

When and how: Use kₑ in Coulomb force, field, and potential; use ε₀ in Gauss law and vacuum capacitance. They encode the same interaction strength because kₑ = 1/(4πε₀).

Watch for: Do not substitute G for kₑ. Convert charges to coulombs and lengths to metres first.

1.2 · Elementary charge, gravity, and charge counting

e=1.602×10−19 C,G=6.674×10−11,q=Ne e=1.602\times10^{-19}\,\mathrm C,\quad G=6.674\times10^{-11},\quad q=Nee=1.602×10−19C,G=6.674×10−11,q=Ne

When and how: Use e to convert an electron count into net charge: N extra electrons gives q = −Ne when N is a positive count. The sheet instead defines N as a signed integer. Use G for gravitational attraction.

Watch for: Neutral means equal positive and negative charge. It does not mean the object contains no charged particles.

1.3 · Conservation and vector reconstruction

Qbefore=Qafter,∣E∣=Ex2+Ey2,θ=atan2⁡(Ey,Ex) Q_{\rm before}=Q_{\rm after},\quad |\mathbf E|=\sqrt{E_x^2+E_y^2},\quad \theta=\operatorname{atan2}(E_y,E_x)Qbefore​=Qafter​,∣E∣=Ex2​+Ey2​​,θ=atan2(Ey​,Ex​)

When and how: Use charge conservation for transfer between objects in an isolated system. After adding electric-field components, use the square root to find magnitude and atan2 to find the correct direction quadrant.

Watch for: A zero vector total may result from cancellation of nonzero contributions. atan2(Ey,Ex) is not simply arctan(Ey/Ex) in every quadrant.

2 · Coulomb force, gravity & superposition

2.1 · Point-charge fields and test-charge force

Ri=r−ri,E(r)=ke∑iqiRi∣Ri∣3,F=q0E\mathbf R_i=\mathbf r-\mathbf r_i,\quad \mathbf E(\mathbf r)=k_e\sum_i\frac{q_i\mathbf R_i}{|\mathbf R_i|^3},\quad \mathbf F=q_0\mathbf ERi​=r−ri​,E(r)=ke​∑i​∣Ri​∣3qi​Ri​​,F=q0​E

When and how: Given charges at coordinates, form each displacement Rᵢ from its source to the observation point. Evaluate each vector contribution, add them, and multiply by q₀ if force is requested. This handles both 1D and 2D.

Watch for: Rᵢ/|Rᵢ|³ includes direction and inverse-square magnitude. A negative source reverses its field; a negative test charge reverses its force relative to the total field.

2.2 · Two-body electric force and gravity

FC=ke∣q1q2∣r2,Fg=Gm1m2r2,Ug=−Gm1m2r F_C=k_e\frac{|q_1q_2|}{r^2},\quad F_g=G\frac{m_1m_2}{r^2},\quad U_g=-\frac{Gm_1m_2}{r}FC​=ke​r2∣q1​q2​∣​,Fg​=Gr2m1​m2​​,Ug​=−rGm1​m2​​

When and how: Use FC for the magnitude between two point charges. Use Fg for gravitational attraction between masses and Ug for their gravitational potential energy with zero at infinity.

Watch for: The absolute value in FC discards direction, so supply attraction or repulsion separately. Ug is negative for positive masses; force and energy have different powers of distance.

3 · Continuous charge & trig substitution

3.1 · Choose a charge element

Q=∫dq,dq=λq dl=σ dA=ρq dV,E=ke∫R^R2dqQ=\int dq,\quad dq=\lambda_q\,dl=\sigma\,dA=\rho_q\,dV,\quad \mathbf E=k_e\int\frac{\hat{\mathbf R}}{R^2}dqQ=∫dq,dq=λq​dl=σdA=ρq​dV,E=ke​∫R2R^​dq

When and how: Use λq dl for a wire, σ dA for a sheet, or ρq dV for a volume. Integrate dq for total charge, or integrate the vector dE for the field at a specified point.

Watch for: These are alternative descriptions of the source, not three densities to multiply together. Distance R and direction generally vary over the source.

3.2 · Field of a finite rod on its perpendicular bisector

Ey=keλqa∫−LLdx(a2+x2)3/2=2keλqLaa2+L2 E_y=k_e\lambda_q a\int_{-L}^{L}\frac{dx}{(a^2+x^2)^{3/2}}=\frac{2k_e\lambda_q L}{a\sqrt{a^2+L^2}}Ey​=ke​λq​a∫−LL​(a2+x2)3/2dx​=aa2+L2​2ke​λq​L​

When and how: Use this integral/result for a uniform rod from −L to L, observed at (0,a). Equal elements cancel horizontal components and reinforce vertical components; the projection supplies the extra factor a/R.

Watch for: Do not use this result at an arbitrary off-axis point. The denominator power 3/2 includes both inverse-square distance and the component projection.

3.3 · Trigonometric substitution

x=atan⁡θ,dx=asec⁡2θ dθ,R=asec⁡θ x=a\tan\theta,\quad dx=a\sec^2\theta\,d\theta,\quad R=a\sec\thetax=atanθ,dx=asec2θdθ,R=asecθ

When and how: When a²+x² occurs under a root, put x = a tanθ. Transform both dx and R, then replace the x bounds with angular bounds. This reduces the rod integral to a cosine integral.

Watch for: θ is the angle from the perpendicular through the rod midpoint. Forgetting the sec²θ factor in dx gives the wrong integral.

3.4 · Evaluate and check the rod integral

Ey=keλqa∫−θ0θ0cos⁡θ dθ,θ0=tan⁡−1(L/a) E_y=\frac{k_e\lambda_q}{a}\int_{-\theta_0}^{\theta_0}\cos\theta\,d\theta,\quad \theta_0=\tan^{-1}(L/a)Ey​=ake​λq​​∫−θ0​θ0​​cosθdθ,θ0​=tan−1(L/a)

When and how: The endpoint angle is θ₀ = arctan(L/a). Integrating cosine between ±θ₀ gives twice sinθ₀; sinθ₀ = L/√(a²+L²) restores the finite-rod result.

Watch for: For L much larger than a, recover an infinite line. For a much larger than L, recover a point charge with Q = 2Lλq.

4 · Flux, Gauss law & standard geometries

4.1 · Flux integral and Gauss law

ΦE=∫SE⋅dA,ΦE=EAcos⁡θ (uniform),∮E⋅dA=Qencϵ0\Phi_E=\int_S\mathbf E\cdot d\mathbf A,\quad \Phi_E=EA\cos\theta\ (\mathrm{uniform}),\quad \oint\mathbf E\cdot d\mathbf A=\frac{Q_{\rm enc}}{\epsilon_0}ΦE​=∫S​E⋅dA,ΦE​=EAcosθ (uniform),∮E⋅dA=ϵ0​Qenc​​

When and how: Use the open-surface integral for flux through any surface and EA cosθ only when the field and angle are uniform. Use the closed integral to relate total outward flux to net enclosed charge.

Watch for: θ is measured from the surface normal. Zero net flux does not imply zero local field. An external charge contributes zero net closed-surface flux.

4.2 · Line, sheet, and spherical symmetry

Eline=λq2πϵ0r,Esheet=σ2ϵ0,Esphere=Qenc(r)4πϵ0r2 E_{\rm line}=\frac{\lambda_q}{2\pi\epsilon_0r},\quad E_{\rm sheet}=\frac{\sigma}{2\epsilon_0},\quad E_{\rm sphere}=\frac{Q_{\rm enc}(r)}{4\pi\epsilon_0r^2}Eline​=2πϵ0​rλq​​,Esheet​=2ϵ0​σ​,Esphere​=4πϵ0​r2Qenc​(r)​

When and how: Choose the line formula for an effectively infinite uniform line, the sheet formula for an infinite nonconducting sheet, and the spherical formula when the charge distribution is spherically symmetric.

Watch for: Gauss law always holds, but these direct field formulas require symmetry. A conducting surface has σ/ε₀ just outside, not σ/(2ε₀).

4.3 · Enclosed charge and uniform solid sphere

Qenc=4π∫0rρq(s)s2ds;ρq=const: Ein=ρqr3ϵ0, Eout=ρqR33ϵ0r2 Q_{\rm enc}=4\pi\int_0^r\rho_q(s)s^2ds;\quad \rho_q=\mathrm{const}:\ E_{\rm in}=\frac{\rho_q r}{3\epsilon_0},\ E_{\rm out}=\frac{\rho_q R^3}{3\epsilon_0r^2}Qenc​=4π∫0r​ρq​(s)s2ds;ρq​=const: Ein​=3ϵ0​ρq​r​, Eout​=3ϵ0​r2ρq​R3​

When and how: For a radially varying density, integrate concentric shells of volume 4πs² ds to the Gaussian radius. For constant density, use the linear interior field and inverse-square exterior field.

Watch for: Inside use only charge within r; outside use the whole sphere of radius R. The two uniform-sphere formulas agree at r = R.

4.4 · Uniform solid cylinder

Ecyl,in=ρqr2ϵ0,Ecyl,out=ρqR22ϵ0r E_{\rm cyl,in}=\frac{\rho_q r}{2\epsilon_0},\quad E_{\rm cyl,out}=\frac{\rho_q R^2}{2\epsilon_0r}Ecyl,in​=2ϵ0​ρq​r​,Ecyl,out​=2ϵ0​rρq​R2​

When and how: Use the interior/exterior expressions for an infinitely long uniformly volume-charged cylinder of radius R. Cylindrical Gaussian area grows with r while enclosed interior charge grows with r².

Watch for: A conducting cylinder is different: the field inside its metal is zero. For coaxial shells, compute the signed enclosed charge separately in each radial region.

5 · Conductors, potential & work

5.1 · Conductor equilibrium

Emetal=0,Et=0,En,out=σ/ϵ0E_{\rm metal}=0,\quad E_t=0,\quad E_{n,\rm out}=\sigma/\epsilon_0Emetal​=0,Et​=0,En,out​=σ/ϵ0​

When and how: Use Emetal = 0 inside conducting material at electrostatic equilibrium. At its surface the tangential component is zero and the outward normal field just outside in vacuum is σ/ε₀.

Watch for: A cavity containing charge can have a field even though the surrounding metal does not. The surface field sign follows the signed surface density.

5.2 · Potential from sources or a field

V=ke∑iqiRi=ke∫dqR,ΔV=−∫abE⋅dl V=k_e\sum_i\frac{q_i}{R_i}=k_e\int\frac{dq}{R},\quad \Delta V=-\int_a^b\mathbf E\cdot d\mathbf lV=ke​∑i​Ri​qi​​=ke​∫Rdq​,ΔV=−∫ab​E⋅dl

When and how: For point charges or a continuous source, sum/integrate scalar potentials with zero at infinity where that reference is valid. If E is known instead, integrate −E·dl between two locations.

Watch for: Potential contributions add with charge signs but without vector components. For an infinite line, use a finite reference potential difference rather than zero at infinity.

5.3 · Potential slope, energy, and charge pairs

E=−∇V,Ex=−∂V∂x,U=qV,U12=keq1q2r \mathbf E=-\nabla V,\quad E_x=-\frac{\partial V}{\partial x},\quad U=qV,\quad U_{12}=\frac{k_eq_1q_2}{r}E=−∇V,Ex​=−∂x∂V​,U=qV,U12​=rke​q1​q2​​

When and how: Use E = −∇V to get a field from a spatial potential function; use the partial derivative for one component. U = qV uses the external potential at a test charge; U12 is pair interaction energy.

Watch for: Do not include a charge’s own potential in qV. If summing pair energies for many charges, count each pair once. Field points toward decreasing V.

5.4 · Work and particle speed

WE=−ΔU=−qΔV,K=12mv2,ΔK=−qΔV W_E=-\Delta U=-q\Delta V,\quad K=\tfrac12mv^2,\quad \Delta K=-q\Delta VWE​=−ΔU=−qΔV,K=21​mv2,ΔK=−qΔV

When and how: Use WE = −qΔV for electric work. If only electric work changes kinetic energy, set ΔK = −qΔV and use K = ½mv² to find a speed or turning point.

Watch for: Work-energy gives speed, not the full velocity direction. If another force does work, include its contribution rather than using ΔK = −qΔV alone.

6 · Useful axial fields & potential shapes

6.1 · Uniform ring on its axis

Ering(z)=keQz(z2+R2)3/2,Vring(z)=keQz2+R2E_{\rm ring}(z)=\frac{k_eQz}{(z^2+R^2)^{3/2}},\quad V_{\rm ring}(z)=\frac{k_eQ}{\sqrt{z^2+R^2}}Ering​(z)=(z2+R2)3/2ke​Qz​,Vring​(z)=z2+R2​ke​Q​

When and how: Use these expressions for a ring of radius R and total charge Q, observed a signed axial distance z from its center. Transverse fields cancel; the axial field changes sign with z.

Watch for: At the center the field is zero but the potential is generally nonzero. Far from the ring both reduce to point-charge behavior.

6.2 · Uniform disk on its axis

Edisk(z)=σ2ϵ0(1−zz2+R2)(z>0) E_{\rm disk}(z)=\frac{\sigma}{2\epsilon_0}\left(1-\frac{z}{\sqrt{z^2+R^2}}\right)\quad(z>0)Edisk​(z)=2ϵ0​σ​(1−z2+R2​z​)(z>0)

When and how: Use the disk field on the positive axis for uniform surface density σ. One derivation adds rings of charge, each contributing its axial field.

Watch for: This formula states z > 0. On the other side reverse the direction appropriately. A disk much wider than z approaches the infinite-sheet result.

6.3 · Force from an interaction-energy curve

Fr=−dUdr;U=C12r12−C6r6⇒Fr=12C12r13−6C6r7 F_r=-\frac{dU}{dr};\quad U=\frac{C_{12}}{r^{12}}-\frac{C_6}{r^6}\Rightarrow F_r=\frac{12C_{12}}{r^{13}}-\frac{6C_6}{r^7}Fr​=−drdU​;U=r12C12​​−r6C6​​⇒Fr​=r1312C12​​−r76C6​​

When and how: Differentiate U with respect to outward separation r and change the sign. In the Lennard–Jones model, the short-range r⁻¹² energy term creates repulsion while the negative r⁻⁶ term creates attraction farther out.

Watch for: Equilibrium is a zero slope, not zero energy. A local minimum is stable. C12 and C6 are material-specific constants, not electric charges.

6.4 · Arc center and solid-sphere potential

Earc,center=2keλqsin⁡αR,Vsolid,in=keQ2R(3−r2R2) E_{\rm arc,center}=\frac{2k_e\lambda_q\sin\alpha}{R},\quad V_{\rm solid,in}=\frac{k_eQ}{2R}\left(3-\frac{r^2}{R^2}\right)Earc,center​=R2ke​λq​sinα​,Vsolid,in​=2Rke​Q​(3−R2r2​)

When and how: For a uniform arc of total angle 2α, use the arc result at its center; symmetry leaves a field along the bisector. For a uniform solid sphere, use the potential result at r < R with zero at infinity.

Watch for: The arc expression gives a signed bisector component for a chosen orientation; use an absolute value for magnitude. The solid-sphere result joins kₑQ/R at the surface.

7 · Capacitors & dielectric response

7.1 · Capacitance definition, plates, and combinations

C=QΔV, Cplate=ϵAd, C∥=∑Ci, 1Cseries=∑1CiC=\frac{Q}{\Delta V},\ C_{\rm plate}=\frac{\epsilon A}{d},\ C_{\parallel}=\sum C_i,\ \frac1{C_{\rm series}}=\sum\frac1{C_i}C=ΔVQ​, Cplate​=dϵA​, C∥​=∑Ci​, Cseries​1​=∑Ci​1​

When and how: Use C = Q/ΔV for charge storage, εA/d for large parallel plates, and the combination rules to simplify networks. Parallel elements share voltage; a simple isolated series chain has equal charge magnitude.

Watch for: C is geometry/material dependent, not proportional to Q when voltage changes on the same ideal capacitor. Use Q as the magnitude on either plate.

7.2 · Spherical and coaxial capacitors

Csphere=4πϵabb−a,Ccoax=2πϵLln⁡(b/a) C_{\rm sphere}=4\pi\epsilon\frac{ab}{b-a},\quad C_{\rm coax}=\frac{2\pi\epsilon L}{\ln(b/a)}Csphere​=4πϵb−aab​,Ccoax​=ln(b/a)2πϵL​

When and how: Use the spherical result for concentric conducting shells of radii a and b; use the coaxial result for long coaxial cylinders of length L. Both assume the intervening dielectric is uniform.

Watch for: a is the smaller radius. Fringing must be negligible for the coaxial expression. Let b tend to infinity to get the capacitance 4πεa of an isolated sphere.

7.3 · Stored energy and energy density

UC=∫0QqCdq=Q22C=12C(ΔV)2,uE=12ϵE2 U_C=\int_0^Q\frac{q}{C}dq=\frac{Q^2}{2C}=\tfrac12C(\Delta V)^2,\quad u_E=\tfrac12\epsilon E^2UC​=∫0Q​Cq​dq=2CQ2​=21​C(ΔV)2,uE​=21​ϵE2

When and how: Integrate q/C as a capacitor is charged to Q to obtain its stored energy. Use the equivalent Q²/(2C) or C(ΔV)²/2 form depending on what is known; uE is energy per unit volume.

Watch for: Decide what stays fixed: with a battery attached ΔV is fixed; disconnected Q is fixed. uE = εE²/2 assumes a linear, nondispersive dielectric in this electrostatic model.

7.4 · Polarization and electric displacement

D=ϵ0E+P=ϵE,P=ϵ0χeE,ϵ=κϵ0=ϵ0(1+χe) \mathbf D=\epsilon_0\mathbf E+\mathbf P=\epsilon\mathbf E,\quad \mathbf P=\epsilon_0\chi_e\mathbf E,\quad \epsilon=\kappa\epsilon_0=\epsilon_0(1+\chi_e)D=ϵ0​E+P=ϵE,P=ϵ0​χe​E,ϵ=κϵ0​=ϵ0​(1+χe​)

When and how: Use P to describe dipole moment per volume and D to separate free charge from bound material charge. In a linear isotropic dielectric P is parallel to E, allowing D = εE.

Watch for: D = εE is a material relation, not a universal law. χe = κ−1 is dimensionless; E has units V/m, while D and P have units C/m².

8 · Dipoles, mechanics & calculus starts

8.1 · Dipole rotation and orientation energy

p=qd,τ=p×E,U=−p⋅E\mathbf p=q\mathbf d,\quad \boldsymbol\tau=\mathbf p\times\mathbf E,\quad U=-\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf Ep=qd,τ=p×E,U=−p⋅E

When and how: Use p = qd for separated equal and opposite charges, τ = p×E for torque, and U = −p·E for orientation energy. A uniform field can give zero net force but a nonzero turning effect.

Watch for: d points from negative to positive charge. Parallel alignment minimizes energy; torque magnitude is pE sinθ and is zero for parallel or antiparallel orientations.

8.2 · Distant dipole fields and potential

Eaxial≃2kep/r3, Eequator≃kep/r3, V≃kepcos⁡θ/r2 E_{\rm axial}\simeq2k_ep/r^3,\ E_{\rm equator}\simeq k_ep/r^3,\ V\simeq k_ep\cos\theta/r^2Eaxial​≃2ke​p/r3, Eequator​≃ke​p/r3, V≃ke​pcosθ/r2

When and how: Use the axial or equatorial field magnitude far from a small dipole, and the potential expression at polar angle θ measured from p. These approximate the difference between two nearly canceling point-charge sources.

Watch for: Require r much larger than the charge separation. The equatorial field points opposite p; the axial field points along p. At the equator V = 0 does not imply E = 0.

8.3 · Newton law and derivative definitions

∑F=ma,a=dvdt,v=dxdt \sum\mathbf F=m\mathbf a,\quad a=\frac{dv}{dt},\quad v=\frac{dx}{dt}∑F=ma,a=dtdv​,v=dtdx​

When and how: Start dynamics with the vector sum of forces equal to ma. Use a = dv/dt and v = dx/dt to move between acceleration, velocity, and position by differentiation or integration.

Watch for: Net force, not just one force, determines acceleration. Initial values are needed when integrating; derivatives of position alone cannot recover those constants.

8.4 · Constant-acceleration motion

a=const: v=vi+at, x=xi+vit+12at2, v2=vi2+2aΔx a=\mathrm{const}:\ v=v_i+at,\ x=x_i+v_it+\tfrac12at^2,\ v^2=v_i^2+2a\Delta xa=const: v=vi​+at, x=xi​+vi​t+21​at2, v2=vi2​+2aΔx

When and how: Use these kinematics formulas only when acceleration is constant along the chosen axis. Choose v = vi+at for time-to-speed, x(t) for displacement with time, and v² = vi²+2aΔx when time is absent.

Watch for: Apply each component separately in 2D. The squared-speed relation loses the velocity sign, so direction must come from the physical motion.

8.5 · Deflection between parallel plates

ay=qE/m,t=ℓ/vx,y=12ayt2 (vy,i=0) a_y=qE/m,\quad t=\ell/v_x,\quad y=\tfrac12a_yt^2\ (v_{y,i}=0)ay​=qE/m,t=ℓ/vx​,y=21​ay​t2 (vy,i​=0)

When and how: For uniform transverse E, compute ay = qE/m. If no horizontal force acts, transit time is plate length ℓ divided by vx. With zero initial vertical speed and displacement, y = ½ayt².

Watch for: This gives displacement while inside the plates. Beyond them, motion is straight at the exit velocity if no other forces act. Include gravity if it is not negligible.

9 · Oscillations, energy & damping

9.1 · Restoring force and natural period

F=−ksx⇒x¨+ω02x=0,ω0=ks/m,T=2π/ω0F=-k_sx\Rightarrow \ddot x+\omega_0^2x=0,\quad \omega_0=\sqrt{k_s/m},\quad T=2\pi/\omega_0F=−ks​x⇒x¨+ω02​x=0,ω0​=ks​/m​,T=2π/ω0​

When and how: Use Hooke law to obtain the SHM equation and ω₀ = √(ks/m). Period is 2π/ω₀. This identifies ideal mass-spring motion and supplies the starting point for a differential-equation derivation.

Watch for: x is measured from equilibrium. ks is spring stiffness, not Coulomb’s constant or wave number. Greater mass increases period; greater stiffness decreases it.

9.2 · SHM position, velocity, and acceleration

x=Acos⁡(ω0t+ϕ), v=−Aω0sin⁡(ω0t+ϕ), a=−ω02x x=A\cos(\omega_0t+\phi),\ v=-A\omega_0\sin(\omega_0t+\phi),\ a=-\omega_0^2xx=Acos(ω0​t+ϕ), v=−Aω0​sin(ω0​t+ϕ), a=−ω02​x

When and how: Use the sinusoidal solution for an undamped oscillator; differentiate to get v and a. At a turning point speed is zero and restoring acceleration has its greatest magnitude.

Watch for: φ sets the initial state. The velocity formula here corresponds to the displayed cosine displacement; do not change one phase convention without changing the derivatives.

9.3 · Energy at an oscillator position

Us=12ksx2,K=12ks(A2−x2),Etot=12ksA2 U_s=\tfrac12k_sx^2,\quad K=\tfrac12k_s(A^2-x^2),\quad E_{\rm tot}=\tfrac12k_sA^2Us​=21​ks​x2,K=21​ks​(A2−x2),Etot​=21​ks​A2

When and how: Use Us for spring energy, K for kinetic energy at position x, and Etot for total energy fixed by amplitude A. This often gives speed without first solving for time.

Watch for: These formulas assume ideal undamped SHM. x must lie between −A and A; K cannot be negative. At equilibrium energy is kinetic; at a turning point it is all spring energy.

9.4 · Pendulum period and driven-oscillator equation

Tpend=2πℓ/g,mx¨+bx˙+ksx=F0cos⁡(Ωt) T_{\rm pend}=2\pi\sqrt{\ell/g},\quad m\ddot x+b\dot x+k_sx=F_0\cos(\Omega t)Tpend​=2πℓ/g​,mx¨+bx˙+ks​x=F0​cos(Ωt)

When and how: Use the pendulum period for small angular oscillations of a simple pendulum. Use the driven differential equation when damping proportional to velocity and an external sinusoidal force are present.

Watch for: Pendulum length ℓ runs from pivot to mass. Large-angle pendulum motion is not described by this amplitude-independent period. b is a damping coefficient, not a radius.

9.5 · Damped frequency and steady driven amplitude

ωd=ω02−(b/2m)2,Adrive=F0(ks−mΩ2)2+(bΩ)2 \omega_d=\sqrt{\omega_0^2-(b/2m)^2},\quad A_{\rm drive}=\frac{F_0}{\sqrt{(k_s-m\Omega^2)^2+(b\Omega)^2}}ωd​=ω02​−(b/2m)2​,Adrive​=(ks​−mΩ2)2+(bΩ)2​F0​​

When and how: Use ωd for free underdamped oscillations when b/(2m) < ω₀. Use Adrive for the steady-state amplitude at forcing frequency Ω, after startup transients have decayed.

Watch for: Free damped motion and forced steady motion are different cases. With damping, the displacement-amplitude resonance is generally slightly below ω₀; at zero damping and exact resonance no finite steady amplitude exists.

10 · Traveling wave: decode & differentiate

10.1 · Decode a sinusoidal traveling wave

y=Acos⁡ψ,ψ=kwx−ωt+ϕ,kw=2πλ,ω=2πfy=A\cos\psi,\quad \psi=k_wx-\omega t+\phi,\quad k_w=\frac{2\pi}{\lambda},\quad \omega=2\pi fy=Acosψ,ψ=kw​x−ωt+ϕ,kw​=λ2π​,ω=2πf

When and how: Compare a supplied wave to A cos(kwx−ωt+φ). Read A, kw, ω, and φ directly; convert kw to wavelength and ω to frequency using 2π. Constant phase identifies direction.

Watch for: For positive kw and ω, minus ωt travels toward +x. A plus sign travels toward −x. Do not confuse angular frequency ω with cycles per second f.

10.2 · Period and propagation speed

T=1f=2πω,vw=ωkw=fλ=T/μ T=\frac1f=\frac{2\pi}{\omega},\quad v_w=\frac\omega{k_w}=f\lambda=\sqrt{\mathcal T/\mu}T=f1​=ω2π​,vw​=kw​ω​=fλ=T/μ​

When and how: Use T = 1/f for period and vw = ω/kw = fλ for waveform speed. For an ideal stretched string use √(𝒯/μ) to connect speed to tension and mass per unit length.

Watch for: The speed relation √(𝒯/μ) is string-specific. Source frequency can change while medium speed remains fixed; wavelength then adjusts.

10.3 · Motion of one point on the string

vy=∂ty=Aωsin⁡ψ,ay=∂t2y=−ω2y v_y=\partial_ty=A\omega\sin\psi,\quad a_y=\partial_t^2y=-\omega^2yvy​=∂t​y=Aωsinψ,ay​=∂t2​y=−ω2y

When and how: Hold x fixed and differentiate with respect to time to get transverse particle velocity and acceleration. Substitute the requested x and t only after taking the derivative.

Watch for: vy is not wave speed vw. The positive sine in vy results from two minus signs: derivative of cosine and derivative of −ωt.

10.4 · Verify the linear wave equation

∂x2y=−kw2y⇒∂t2y=vw2∂x2y \partial_x^2y=-k_w^2y\Rightarrow \boxed{\partial_t^2y=v_w^2\partial_x^2y}∂x2​y=−kw2​y⇒∂t2​y=vw2​∂x2​y​

When and how: Differentiate twice in space and time. The same y appears with factors −kw² and −ω², yielding ∂t²y = vw²∂x²y when vw = ω/kw.

Watch for: This verifies the harmonic solution. General traveling shapes f(x−vwt) also satisfy the constant-speed wave equation; the PDE does not require a sinusoidal source.

10.5 · Derive the string PDE and calculate mean power

T ∂x2y dx=μ dx ∂t2y,P‾=12μω2A2vw \mathcal T\,\partial_x^2y\,dx=\mu\,dx\,\partial_t^2y,\quad \overline P=\tfrac12\mu\omega^2 A^2v_wT∂x2​ydx=μdx∂t2​y,P=21​μω2A2vw​

When and how: For a short string element dx, small-slope tension forces give net transverse force 𝒯 yxx dx and mass μdx times acceleration. Cancel dx to derive the PDE. Use mean power for a sinusoidal traveling string wave.

Watch for: Assume uniform tension and density, small slopes, negligible loss. Doubling amplitude quadruples mean power at fixed frequency and medium.

11 · Boundaries, interference & standing waves

11.1 · Phase difference and interference amplitude

Δψ=kwΔx−ωΔt+Δϕ,Asum=2A∣cos⁡(Δψ/2)∣\Delta\psi=k_w\Delta x-\omega\Delta t+\Delta\phi,\quad A_{\rm sum}=2A|\cos(\Delta\psi/2)|Δψ=kw​Δx−ωΔt+Δϕ,Asum​=2A∣cos(Δψ/2)∣

When and how: Use the phase-difference expression to compare positions, times, or initial phases. For two equal-amplitude, same-frequency coherent waves, the cosine factor gives resultant amplitude.

Watch for: Constructive interference has phase difference 2πm; destructive has (2m+1)π. The amplitude expression is not valid for arbitrary unequal amplitudes.

11.2 · Standing waves and two matching ends

y1+y2=2Asin⁡(kwx)cos⁡(ωt),fn=nvw2L (n=1,2,…) y_1+y_2=2A\sin(k_wx)\cos(\omega t),\quad f_n=\frac{nv_w}{2L}\ (n=1,2,\ldots)y1​+y2​=2Asin(kw​x)cos(ωt),fn​=2Lnvw​​ (n=1,2,…)

When and how: Add equal opposite-traveling waves to get stationary nodes and antinodes. Use fn = nvw/(2L) for a string fixed at both ends or a pipe open at both ends, neglecting end corrections.

Watch for: String fixed ends are displacement nodes. Open pipe ends are displacement antinodes and pressure nodes. Node spacing is λ/2; adjacent node-to-antinode distance is λ/4.

11.3 · One closed pipe end and beats

fn=nvw4L (n=1,3,5,…),fbeat=∣f1−f2∣ f_n=\frac{nv_w}{4L}\ (n=1,3,5,\ldots),\quad f_{\rm beat}=|f_1-f_2|fn​=4Lnvw​​ (n=1,3,5,…),fbeat​=∣f1​−f2​∣

When and how: Use odd-n quarter-wave modes for an open-closed pipe. Use the absolute frequency difference for the slow loudness modulation produced by nearby frequencies.

Watch for: The third harmonic of an open-closed pipe is n = 3; n = 2 is absent in the ideal model. Beat frequency is not the carrier pitch.

11.4 · Refraction and critical angle

n1sin⁡θ1=n2sin⁡θ2,n=c/v,sin⁡θc=n2/n1 n_1\sin\theta_1=n_2\sin\theta_2,\quad n=c/v,\quad \sin\theta_c=n_2/n_1n1​sinθ1​=n2​sinθ2​,n=c/v,sinθc​=n2​/n1​

When and how: Use Snell law for refraction at a stationary interface. With n = c/v, entering a higher-index medium bends toward the normal. Critical angle applies when crossing from higher to lower index.

Watch for: Measure both angles from the normal. Frequency stays continuous. For sound, use sinθ₁/v₁ = sinθ₂/v₂ rather than assuming optical indices; total internal reflection requires a valid critical-angle geometry.

11.5 · String-junction reflection and transmission

Zj=T/vj,rA=Z1−Z2Z1+Z2,tA=2Z1Z1+Z2 Z_j=\mathcal T/v_j,\quad r_A=\frac{Z_1-Z_2}{Z_1+Z_2},\quad t_A=\frac{2Z_1}{Z_1+Z_2}Zj​=T/vj​,rA​=Z1​+Z2​Z1​−Z2​​,tA​=Z1​+Z2​2Z1​​

When and how: Use these signed displacement-amplitude ratios at a junction of two ideal strings under the same tension. Incident motion travels from medium 1 toward medium 2; Z = 𝒯/v is the string impedance.

Watch for: A negative rA means inversion. Amplitude ratios are not energy fractions: reflected power fraction is rA² and transmitted is (Z₂/Z₁)tA². A transmission amplitude greater than one need not violate energy conservation.

12 · Sound, levels & Doppler

12.1 · Sound speed and intensity

vs=B/ρm,I=P4πr2=prms2ρmvs=12ρmvsω2sm2v_s=\sqrt{B/\rho_m},\quad I=\frac{P}{4\pi r^2}=\frac{p_{\rm rms}^2}{\rho_mv_s}=\tfrac12\rho_mv_s\omega^2s_m^2vs​=B/ρm​​,I=4πr2P​=ρm​vs​prms2​​=21​ρm​vs​ω2sm2​

When and how: Use √(B/ρm) for longitudinal sound in a fluid. Use P/(4πr²) for an isotropic source without absorption; use the pressure or displacement forms for a progressive sinusoidal sound wave.

Watch for: B here is bulk modulus, not magnetic field. The equality between pressure intensity and displacement intensity assumes the same medium and a traveling wave.

12.2 · Pressure amplitude, RMS, and level

pm=ρmvsωsm,prms=pm/2,β=10log⁡10(I/I0) p_m=\rho_mv_s\omega s_m,\quad p_{\rm rms}=p_m/\sqrt2,\quad \beta=10\log_{10}(I/I_0)pm​=ρm​vs​ωsm​,prms​=pm​/2​,β=10log10​(I/I0​)

When and how: Convert sinusoidal pressure amplitude to RMS by dividing by √2. Relate pressure amplitude to particle displacement through ρmvsωsm. Use β to express intensity relative to I₀.

Watch for: Do not substitute peak pressure where RMS is required. I₀ is the conventional 10⁻¹² W/m² reference; a negative dB level simply means below that reference.

12.3 · Compare two sound levels

Δβ=10log⁡10(I2/I1)=20log⁡10(p2/p1) \Delta\beta=10\log_{10}(I_2/I_1)=20\log_{10}(p_2/p_1)Δβ=10log10​(I2​/I1​)=20log10​(p2​/p1​)

When and how: For intensity ratios use 10 log10; for pressure ratios in the same acoustic impedance use 20 log10 because intensity is proportional to pressure squared.

Watch for: Pressure tripled means intensity multiplied by nine, and both calculations give +9.54 dB. The pressure-ratio equality needs the same medium/impedance.

12.4 · Doppler shift and induction reminder

fo=fsvs+vovs−vsrc,Eind=−dΦBdt,ΦB=∫B⋅dA f_o=f_s\frac{v_s+v_o}{v_s-v_{\rm src}},\quad \mathcal E_{\rm ind}=-\frac{d\Phi_B}{dt},\quad \Phi_B=\int\mathbf B\cdot d\mathbf Afo​=fs​vs​−vsrc​vs​+vo​​,Eind​=−dtdΦB​​,ΦB​=∫B⋅dA

When and how: Use Doppler for source/observer motion relative to the sound medium: positive vo points toward the source, positive vsrc toward the observer. The other two expressions describe magnetic flux and induced emf when flux changes.

Watch for: Approach raises observed frequency. The sound formula assumes classical, subsonic motion along the line of propagation. Faraday’s minus sign expresses opposition to flux change; the displayed emf is for one loop (multiply by turns for identical linked flux).
